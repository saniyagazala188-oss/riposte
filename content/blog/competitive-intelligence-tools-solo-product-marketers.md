When I was a PMM, competitive intelligence was the job that never fit in the week. Everyone wanted the latest on competitors, collecting it meant checking pages by hand every Monday, and most "best CI tools" lists recommended platforms built for much bigger teams.

So this guide is for the one-person version of the job: a solo PMM, or a founder doing the marketing, at an early-stage B2B SaaS company. I'll cover what each type of tool is good at, what to look for, and a routine that fits in one person's week. I build one of these tools, Riposte, so I'll be clear about where it fits and where it doesn't.

## Why enterprise CI platforms are a lot for one person

Klue and Crayon are the two best known competitive intelligence platforms, and both are strong at what they're built for. That is the key point: they're built for revenue teams.

- **Klue** describes itself as a competitive enablement platform for B2B revenue teams. Its Compete Agent gathers insights from your CRM, sales calls, win-loss data and public sources, battlecards are created and refreshed with AI, and a win-loss program runs buyer interviews.
- **Crayon** describes itself as a competitive intelligence platform focused on helping sales win. Crayon AI summarizes competitor news and scores how important each change is, battlecards show up in tools like Salesforce, Slack and Highspot, and reports cover win/loss and influenced revenue.

Neither publishes prices, and both start with a demo. Much of the value sits in features a solo PMM at an early-stage company may not use yet: battlecards inside the CRM, deal support for a large sales team, win-loss interviews and revenue reporting. They also work best when someone owns the program and curates it every week.

If that's not your situation yet, you're likely to pay for more than you use. I've compared the two side by side in [Crayon vs Klue](/blog/crayon-vs-klue), and kept separate pages on [Klue alternatives](/alternatives/klue) and [Crayon alternatives](/alternatives/crayon).

:::tip The question that decides it
Ask: "Do I need to run a competitive program for a sales team, or do I need to know what changed and respond fast?" The first points to an enterprise platform. The second points to something lighter.
:::

## What page monitors do, and what they leave to you

Page monitors like Visualping sit at the other end. You pick the pages, it checks them on a schedule and tells you when something changes. Visualping now writes an AI summary of each change, and you can set your own criteria so it only alerts you about changes that matter, like a pricing change or a new feature. It has a free plan for a few pages.

That makes it a cheap, good start for one or two pricing pages. What it leaves to you:

- **Finding the pages.** You add each URL yourself, for every competitor.
- **Judging the change for your product.** A price rise can be a threat for one company and an opening for another. The monitor doesn't know your positioning.
- **The response.** Updating the battlecard, briefing sales or changing your comparison page is still a blank page.

For a solo PMM, those three steps are where the week goes.

## What to look for in a CI tool when you're a team of one

Use this as a checklist in any demo or free trial:

1. **It finds the right pages on its own.** Pricing, release notes, blog, blog feed and sitemap, from just the competitor's website.
2. **It checks daily, without you.** You shouldn't have to remember to run anything.
3. **It filters noise.** Changed dates, cookie banners and rotating testimonials shouldn't reach you.
4. **It sorts changes by impact.** High, medium and low, so you know what to read first.
5. **It explains changes for your product.** Not "they changed pricing", but what that means for your deals.
6. **It drafts the response.** A battlecard update, a talk track or a post you can edit, not just an alert.
7. **It reaches you where you work.** Email or Slack for the important changes, and a weekly digest for the rest.
8. **Setup takes minutes, and you can try it first.** No onboarding project and no sales call before you see it work.

If you don't have a battlecard yet, start one now with the [free battlecard generator](/tools/battlecard-generator). It drafts a one-page card from a competitor's website, and you'll know what good looks like before you judge any tool.

## Track what AI assistants recommend, too

Buyers don't only search Google now. Some ask an AI assistant "what's the best tool for…" and get a short list of names. If your competitors are on that list and you aren't, you lose that buyer before they reach your site.

What to track:

- **The buying questions** your customers would ask, written without brand names.
- **Which brands the answers name**, and how often you show up next to competitors.
- **Which sites the answers rely on**, because those are the places worth getting listed or cited on.

Riposte asks your buyers' questions in Gemini with Google Search every week and records the brands named and the sites cited. Each gap can become a content brief. AI answers change from run to run, so treat this as a trend, not an exact score.

## A lean weekly CI routine for a solo PMM

This is the routine I'd run as a team of one, with or without a tool:

1. **Pick 3 to 5 competitors.** The ones you lose deals to, not everyone in your category.
2. **Watch the pages that change deals.** Pricing, release notes or changelog, blog, and sitemap for new pages.
3. **Read high-impact changes the day they happen.** Leave the rest for a weekly review.
4. **Ask one question per change:** does this change what sales says on a call? If not, log it and move on.
5. **If yes, update the battlecard that week** and post one line in the sales channel saying what changed.
6. **Once a month, check AI answers** for your top buying questions, and note who gets named.

The log matters more than it looks. After a couple of months, you'll see patterns, like a competitor that changes pricing every quarter or always launches before a big event.

## Wrapping up

For a solo product marketer, the best competitive intelligence tool is the one that gives you back the checking and the first draft, so your time goes into the judgment calls. Enterprise platforms like Klue and Crayon are worth it once you run a CI program for a large sales team. Until then, start light: a few competitors, the pages that change deals, and a weekly routine you can actually keep.
