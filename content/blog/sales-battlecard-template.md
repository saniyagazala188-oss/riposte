Every sales team I've worked with had battlecards. Very few had battlecards that were right.

The card says the competitor costs $49. They moved to $59 two months ago. The rep quotes the old number on a call, the buyer corrects them, and now the rep looks like they don't know the market. After that, they stop opening the cards.

So this post is about two things: what to put on a battlecard, and how to keep it true.

## What is a sales battlecard?

A sales battlecard is a short guide that helps a rep win a deal against one specific competitor. It answers the questions a rep has five minutes before a call: Who is this? Why do buyers pick them? Why do they pick us? What will the buyer say, and what do I say back?

It is not a full competitor analysis. That is a long document for product marketing and leadership. A battlecard is the one page that a rep actually reads.

:::tip The two-minute test
If a rep can't read the card in two minutes before a call, it's too long. Cut it until they can.
:::

## The 8 parts of a good battlecard

| Section | What to write | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Quick take** | One line: who they are and how they position themselves | "All-in-one CI platform for large sales teams" |
| **2. Who they sell to** | Company size, industry and buyer role they target | "Mid-market and enterprise, sold to PMM and sales enablement" |
| **3. Why we win** | Three reasons buyers pick you over them, in the buyer's words | "Up and running in a day, not a quarter" |
| **4. Why we lose** | The honest reasons you lose to them | "They have more integrations" |
| **5. Pricing** | Their plans and prices, with the date you checked | "Pro $59/mo (checked 12 Oct)" |
| **6. Objections and answers** | What buyers say, and a short answer for each | "They're cheaper" → your answer |
| **7. Trap-setting questions** | Questions that steer the buyer toward your strengths | "How long did setup take with your last tool?" |
| **8. Proof** | A customer quote, case study or number that backs you up | "Acme switched and cut prep time by half" |

Add two things at the top of every card: the **owner** and a **last checked** date. Those two lines do more for trust than anything else on the card.

## Free sales battlecard template

Copy this into Notion, Google Docs or Confluence. One card per competitor. Want a first draft filled in for you? Try the [free battlecard generator](/tools/battlecard-generator): enter a competitor's website and get these sections drafted from their own pages.

```
BATTLECARD: [Competitor name]
Owner: [name]          Last checked: [date]

1. QUICK TAKE
   [One line on who they are and how they position themselves]

2. WHO THEY SELL TO
   [Company size, industry, buyer role]

3. WHY WE WIN
   - [Reason 1, in the buyer's words]
   - [Reason 2]
   - [Reason 3]

4. WHY WE LOSE
   - [Honest reason 1]
   - [Honest reason 2]

5. PRICING (checked [date])
   [Plan]: [price]   [Plan]: [price]
   Link: [their pricing page]

6. OBJECTIONS AND ANSWERS
   "[What the buyer says]"  →  [What you say back]
   "[What the buyer says]"  →  [What you say back]

7. TRAP-SETTING QUESTIONS
   - [Question that points to your strength]
   - [Question that points to their weak spot]

8. PROOF
   [Customer quote, case study or number]

RECENT CHANGES
   [Date]: [What changed and what it means for us]
```

:::note Why "Recent changes" is at the bottom
It's the section reps check first once they know the card. A short dated list tells them the card is alive, and tells them what's new since their last deal.
:::

## Why most battlecards go out of date

Writing the first version is the easy part. Competitors don't stand still: they change prices, launch features, rewrite their homepage and publish new comparison pages. Each change can make one line on your card wrong.

Most teams find out late, usually from a buyer on a call. By then the damage is done.

There are three common reasons:

- **No owner.** When everyone owns the card, no one updates it.
- **No trigger.** The card only gets updated during a quarterly review, not when the competitor actually changes.
- **No time.** Checking five competitors' pricing pages, changelogs and blogs every week takes hours that no one has.

## How to keep battlecards up to date

A simple routine that works:

1. **Give each card one owner** and put their name at the top.
2. **Watch the pages that change cards:** pricing, changelog or release notes, blog, and news. These four cover most of what makes a card wrong.
3. **Update within a week** of a change, and add a dated line under "Recent changes".
4. **Tell sales what changed** in the channel they already read, with one line on what to say now.
5. **Review every card fully once a quarter**, and archive cards for competitors you no longer see in deals.

## Common battlecard mistakes

- **Trashing the competitor.** Buyers trust reps who are fair. Say what they're good at, then say why you're a better fit.
- **Feature lists instead of answers.** Reps don't need 40 features. They need the three that win deals and the words to say them.
- **Hiding why you lose.** If the card only says good things, reps get surprised on calls. Be honest about your weak spots and give them a way to handle each one.
- **Prices with no date.** A price without a "checked on" date can't be trusted. Always add the date.
- **Cards no one can find.** Put a link to the cards in the CRM, pinned in the sales channel, and in the deal review template.

## Wrapping up

A battlecard is a promise to your sales team: "this is true today". Keep it to one page, put a name and a date on it, and update it whenever the competitor changes. The template above gets you the first part. Watching competitors every day is the part to automate.
